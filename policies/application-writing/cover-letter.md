# Cover letter: make the reader's problem the subject

The hiring manager is scanning for one thing: will this person take a problem off my plate? The letter's job is to make them think "they have already done the thing I need." Name their problem in their words, show the result the candidate already delivered on that same kind of problem, and stop. Let the reader connect the dots.

## Never

- Never announce the application ("I am writing to apply", "I'm excited to apply", "Please accept my application").
- Never open by restating the posting back to the reader ("The role calls for…", "You are looking for…"). They wrote it; they know.
- Never say or imply "you are hiring because". Speak to the need; don't narrate your guess about it.
- Never praise the company in generic terms ("innovative leader", "mission I deeply admire"). If you mention the company, mention something specific and sourced: a product, a launch, a customer, a stated goal.
- Never restate the résumé line by line, and never state the same fact twice in one letter.
- Never promise results, availability, relocation, or anything the candidate has not stated.
- Never talk the candidate down. No "while I haven't…", no "my experience is adjacent to…", no "I would not present this as…", no comparing their tools or level unfavorably with the posting. A stretch application leads with what transfers and lets the reader decide.
- No salutation, sign-off, or signature in the paragraphs; the system adds them.

## Choose a shape (`strategy.letterShape`)

- **PROBLEM_PROOF**: open with the specific problem this role exists to solve, made concrete, then the candidate's proof on that problem.
- **STORY_LED**: open inside one specific moment from a story (the situation and what the candidate did), then connect it to this role.
- **RESULTS_LED**: open with the strongest relevant result in one sentence, then how they got it.
- **CAREER_BRIDGE**: for a change of field, open with the throughline from what they have done to what this role needs.

Pick the shape the evidence supports. Vary it across applications; do not reuse any opening from `recentOpenings`.

## The opening sentence

It must be something only this candidate could write to this employer. Good patterns:

- Company fact → what it means for the work → the candidate's version of that work. ("[Employer] is [specific sourced move]. For a [role], that means [the concrete work]. I've been doing the ground-level version of that at [employer]: [specific].")
- The concrete problem, then the proof. ("[Concrete problem the role solves, in plain words]. At [employer], that problem was [specific situation], and I [what they did].")
- A specific moment. ("[Situation in one sentence]. [What the candidate did about it].")

## Paragraph plan

1. **Hook (40–80 words):** the need made concrete and why this employer. Use company research when you have it.
2. **Proof (one or two paragraphs):** one or two stories or results matched to the top requirements, with numbers. Show judgment: what the candidate noticed, chose, or did that an average person would not have. Tell it as a short sequence of actions, not a list of duties.
3. **Optional:** the second requirement, or how the candidate's way of working carries over, grounded in what they have actually done. No promises.
4. **Close (one or two sentences):** plain and specific about what they would like to talk about.
   - Good: "I'd like to walk you through how the dispatch dashboard came together and where that approach could help your practices."
   - Good: "If it's useful, I can show you the before-and-after numbers from the intake rebuild."
   - Bad: "I can discuss the experience described above in relation to the role." / "Thank you for your time and consideration." / "I look forward to hearing from you." / "I would welcome the opportunity to contribute."

## Length and texture

- 260–340 words is the target; 200–400 is the hard range. Three to five paragraphs. If the proof is thin, use a second story or a second result rather than filler.
- Name the employer naturally at least once.
- Include at least one concrete detail nobody would fake: a number that is not round, a named tool or customer type, a real constraint.
- First person, plain, confident. A little personality is welcome; hype is not.
- Connective sentences that only relate the candidate's cited work to the cited role ("That is the same coordination problem, with more clinicians") are fine. New facts are not.
